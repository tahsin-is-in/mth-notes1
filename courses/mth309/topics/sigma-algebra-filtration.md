## Why this topic matters

Stochastic calculus is built on the idea of "information available at time $t$." A $\sigma$-algebra is the precise mathematical object that captures "which events I can currently tell apart," and a filtration tracks how that information grows over time — without this, phrases like "adapted process" or "$\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal F_t]$" have no rigorous meaning.

## Prerequisites

- Basic probability (sample spaces, events)
- Set operations (union, intersection, complement)

:::eli5
Imagine you're told the outcome of some random experiment gradually, piece by piece. A $\sigma$-algebra is the precise list of yes/no questions you're currently able to answer about the outcome, based on what you've been told so far. A **filtration** is just this list of questions, growing over time as more information arrives — you never *forget* how to answer a question you could already answer, you only gain more.
:::

## Formal definition

:::formal Sigma-Algebra
A collection $\mathcal F$ of subsets of a sample space $\Omega$ is a **$\sigma$-algebra** if:
1. $\Omega \in \mathcal F$
2. $A\in\mathcal F \implies A^c \in \mathcal F$ (closed under complement)
3. $A_1,A_2,\dots\in\mathcal F \implies \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n \in \mathcal F$ (closed under countable union)
:::

:::formal Filtration
A **filtration** $\{\mathcal F_t\}_{t\ge0}$ is a family of $\sigma$-algebras on $\Omega$ with $\mathcal F_s \subseteq \mathcal F_t$ whenever $s\le t$ — information never decreases over time.
:::

:::theorem Generated Sigma-Algebra
For any collection $\mathcal C$ of subsets of $\Omega$, there is a smallest $\sigma$-algebra containing $\mathcal C$, denoted $\sigma(\mathcal C)$ — the intersection of all $\sigma$-algebras containing $\mathcal C$.
:::

## Worked examples

:::example Easy
Roll a die: $\Omega=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$. The $\sigma$-algebra generated by the event $\{1\}$ is $\sigma(\{1\}) = \{\emptyset, \{1\}, \{2,3,4,5,6\}, \Omega\}$ — the smallest collection containing $\{1\}$ that is closed under complement and (finite, here) unions.
:::

:::example Standard university level
Continuing the die example: the $\sigma$-algebra generated by **both** $\{1\}$ and $\{5\}$ must contain $\{1\},\{5\}$, their complements, their union $\{1,5\}$, its complement $\{2,3,4,6\}$, and so on — working it out gives $\sigma(\{1\},\{5\}) = \{\emptyset,\{1\},\{5\},\{1,5\},\{2,3,4,6\},\{2,3,4,5,6\},\{1,2,3,4,6\},\Omega\}$, 8 sets in total. This $\sigma$-algebra can answer "was it a 1?" and "was it a 5?" but cannot distinguish, say, $\{2\}$ from $\{3\}$.
:::

:::example Exam level
For $\Omega=\{a,b,c\}$: list all $\sigma$-algebras on $\Omega$. Since $|\Omega|=3$ is small, they are: the trivial one $\{\emptyset,\Omega\}$; the three generated by a single point, e.g. $\{\emptyset,\{a\},\{b,c\},\Omega\}$ (and similarly for $b$, $c$); and the full power set $2^\Omega$ (8 subsets) — five $\sigma$-algebras in total. Checking whether $\{\emptyset,\Omega,\{a\},\{a,c\}\}$ is a $\sigma$-algebra: it fails, since $\{a,c\}^c=\{b\}$ is missing.
:::

:::example Difficult
For a two-period binomial stock model with $S_0$ known, $S_1\in\{u S_0, dS_0\}$, and $S_2$ branching further from each: show $S_1$ is $\mathcal F_1$-measurable, where $\mathcal F_1=\sigma(S_1)$. By construction, $\mathcal F_1$ is defined as the smallest $\sigma$-algebra making $S_1$ measurable — the events "did the stock go up in period 1?" and "did it go down?" are exactly the atoms of $\mathcal F_1$, and $S_1$ is constant on each atom, which is precisely the definition of measurability.
:::

:::counterexample
**Statement that looks true:** "Any collection of subsets closed under complement is a $\sigma$-algebra, as long as it contains $\Omega$."

**Counterexample:** $\{\Omega,\emptyset,\{a\},\{b,c\}\}$ on $\Omega=\{a,b,c,d\}$ — wait, this misses $d$ entirely in any set, so it isn't even a valid partition-style collection; take instead $\mathcal F=\{\Omega,\emptyset,\{a\},\{a,c\}\}$ on $\Omega=\{a,b,c\}$: it's closed under complement only if $\{a,c\}^c=\{b\}\in\mathcal F$, which it is **not**.

**Why it fails:** closure under complement is necessary but not sufficient — closure under countable union must be checked too (and, working backward, complements of unions), so a collection can look complement-closed for its listed elements while missing derived sets.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake
Assuming $\sigma(\{A\})$ for a single event $A$ is just $\{A\}$ — it must also include $\emptyset$, $A^c$, and $\Omega$, giving four sets minimum, not one.
:::

:::mistake
Confusing a $\sigma$-algebra with a partition — a $\sigma$-algebra determines a partition (its atoms), but the $\sigma$-algebra itself is the whole collection of *unions* of atoms, which is much larger than the partition itself.
:::

## Applications

:::application
**Finance:** in the binomial stock model, $\mathcal F_t$ represents exactly the market information available to a trader at time $t$ — every trading strategy must be $\mathcal F_t$-measurable (i.e. "adapted"), since a trader cannot base today's decision on tomorrow's stock price.
:::

## Exam-ready answer

:::examready
A $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal F$ on $\Omega$ contains $\Omega$, is closed under complement, and closed under countable union (hence also countable intersection, by De Morgan). A filtration is an increasing family $\{\mathcal F_t\}$ of $\sigma$-algebras representing growing information over time. $\sigma(\mathcal C)$, the $\sigma$-algebra generated by a collection $\mathcal C$, is built by closing $\mathcal C$ under complements and unions until no new sets are produced.
:::

## Viva preparation

:::viva
**Q: Why can't information in a filtration decrease over time?**
Because once you've observed something, you can't "unobserve" it — mathematically, $\mathcal F_s\subseteq\mathcal F_t$ for $s\le t$ encodes that every question answerable at time $s$ is still answerable at time $t$.

**Q: What's the smallest $\sigma$-algebra on any $\Omega$?**
$\{\emptyset,\Omega\}$, the trivial $\sigma$-algebra — it can only ever say "did *something* happen" (always yes) or "did nothing happen from the empty event" (always no), distinguishing nothing.

**Common examiner trap:** asking for $\sigma(\{A\})$ and expecting just $\{A\}$ or $\{A, A^c\}$ as the answer — always include $\emptyset$ and $\Omega$ explicitly.
:::
