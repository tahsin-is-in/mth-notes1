## Why this topic matters

A group is the minimal structure needed to talk about "symmetry" and "invertible combination" abstractly. Once you strip away everything except closure, associativity, an identity, and inverses, the same theorems apply to numbers, permutations, matrices, and symmetries of geometric objects simultaneously.

## Prerequisites

- Binary operations and basic set theory
- Familiarity with $\mathbb Z$, $\mathbb R$, and matrices as motivating examples

:::eli5
A group is a set of "moves" you can combine, where combining two moves gives another valid move (closure), the order you group combinations in doesn't matter (associativity), there's a "do nothing" move (identity), and every move can be undone (inverse). Think of the rotations of a square: rotate by 90°, 180°, 270°, or not at all — combine any two and you get another one on the list, and every rotation can be undone by rotating the other way.
:::

## Formal definition

:::formal Group
A **group** is a set $G$ with a binary operation $*$ such that:
1. **Closure:** $a*b\in G$ for all $a,b\in G$.
2. **Associativity:** $(a*b)*c=a*(b*c)$.
3. **Identity:** $\exists e\in G$ with $e*a=a*e=a$ for all $a$.
4. **Inverses:** for each $a$, $\exists a^{-1}\in G$ with $a*a^{-1}=a^{-1}*a=e$.

If also $a*b=b*a$ for all $a,b$, $G$ is **abelian**.
:::

:::theorem Subgroup Test
A non-empty subset $H\subseteq G$ is a subgroup iff $a*b^{-1}\in H$ for all $a,b\in H$.
:::

:::proof
($\Rightarrow$) If $H$ is a subgroup, $b\in H \implies b^{-1}\in H$ (subgroup has inverses), so $a*b^{-1}\in H$ by closure of $H$.

($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $a*b^{-1}\in H$ for all $a,b\in H$ ($H$ non-empty, so pick some $a_0\in H$). Taking $a=b=a_0$: $e=a_0*a_0^{-1}\in H$. Taking $a=e,\,b$ arbitrary in $H$: $e*b^{-1}=b^{-1}\in H$, so $H$ is closed under inverses. Then for $a,b\in H$, $b^{-1}\in H$, so $a*(b^{-1})^{-1}=a*b\in H$: closure. Associativity is inherited from $G$. So $H$ is a group under $*$. $\blacksquare$
:::

**Why this shortcut matters:** it replaces checking all four group axioms with a single condition — closure and inverses fall out automatically.

## Worked examples

:::example Easy
$(\mathbb Z,+)$ is an abelian group with identity $0$; $\{0,\pm2,\pm4,\dots\}$ is a subgroup (even integers) by the subgroup test: for even $a,b$, $a-b$ is even.
:::

:::example Standard university level
$H=\{0,2,4\}\subseteq \mathbb Z_6$ (integers mod 6, under addition): for any $a,b\in H$, $a-b \pmod 6 \in\{0,2,4\}$, so $H\le\mathbb Z_6$ by the subgroup test. This $H$ is exactly the cyclic subgroup generated by $2$.
:::

:::example Exam level
Show $S_3$ (symmetric group on 3 symbols, $|S_3|=6$) is non-abelian. Let $\sigma=(1\,2)$, $\tau=(1\,3)$. Compute $\sigma\tau = (1\,3\,2)$ but $\tau\sigma=(1\,2\,3)$ — these are different permutations, so $\sigma\tau\ne\tau\sigma$, confirming $S_3$ is non-abelian.
:::

:::example Difficult
Let $H(F)$ be the set of $2\times2$ upper unitriangular matrices $\begin{pmatrix}1&x\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ over a field $F$. Show this is a group under matrix multiplication and find its center. Multiplication gives $\begin{pmatrix}1&x\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&y\\0&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&x+y\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, so $H(F)\cong (F,+)$ — in particular $H(F)$ is already abelian, so its center is all of $H(F)$.
:::

:::counterexample
**Statement that looks true:** "Every non-empty subset of a group closed under the group operation is a subgroup."

**Counterexample:** $H=\{1,2,3,\dots\}\subset(\mathbb Z,+)$ (positive integers) is closed under addition, but $0\notin H$ and no element has an additive inverse inside $H$ — $H$ is **not** a subgroup.

**Why it fails:** closure alone omits the identity and inverse requirements — the subgroup test's real content is that both must be checked (or verified in one combined step via $a*b^{-1}\in H$).
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake
Verifying only closure and calling a subset a subgroup — as the counterexample shows, closure alone is not sufficient; the identity and inverses must also be present.
:::

:::mistake
Assuming a group of small order must be abelian — true for order $\le 5$ and for order $4$ specifically (provable), but false in general (e.g. $S_3$, order $6$, is non-abelian).
:::

## Connections to other topics

:::connection
The subgroup test here is the tool used immediately in Cosets and Lagrange's Theorem (MTH304-CH02) to verify that cosets partition the group — every later structural theorem about groups builds on correctly identifying subgroups.

## Applications

:::application
**Cryptography and coding theory:** finite groups (particularly $\mathbb Z_n$ and groups of units mod $n$) underlie RSA encryption and error-correcting codes — Lagrange's theorem (next topic) directly explains why Fermat's Little Theorem holds, which RSA depends on.
:::

## Exam-ready answer

:::examready
A group needs closure, associativity, an identity, and inverses. To show $H\subseteq G$ is a subgroup, use the one-line subgroup test: $H\ne\emptyset$ and $a*b^{-1}\in H$ for all $a,b\in H$ — this single check replaces verifying all four axioms separately.
:::

## Viva preparation

:::viva
**Q: Does closure alone make a subset a subgroup?**
No — you also need the identity element and inverses to be present in the subset, which is exactly what the combined subgroup test checks in one step.

**Q: Give an example of a non-abelian group.**
$S_3$, the symmetric group on 3 symbols — composition of permutations does not commute in general.

**Common examiner trap:** asking whether a specific small subset (often missing the identity, or missing inverses for a non-obvious element) is a subgroup — always test the subgroup criterion explicitly rather than eyeballing it.
:::
