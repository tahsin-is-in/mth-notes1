## Why this topic matters

Lagrange's Theorem is a pure counting argument with enormous reach — it instantly rules out most possible subgroup sizes, explains why every group of prime order is cyclic, and underlies Fermat's Little Theorem (and, by extension, RSA cryptography).

## Prerequisites

- Groups and subgroups
- Equivalence relations / partitions

:::eli5
Imagine cutting a group $G$ into equal-sized tiles, where each tile is a "shifted copy" of a subgroup $H$ (a coset). Every tile has exactly $|H|$ elements, and the tiles perfectly cover $G$ with no overlap. If $|H|$ tiles of that size fit perfectly with no leftover space, then $|H|$ must divide $|G|$ — that's the whole theorem.
:::

## Formal definition

:::formal Coset
For $H\le G$ and $a\in G$, the **left coset** of $H$ by $a$ is $aH=\{a*h : h\in H\}$.
:::

:::theorem Properties of Cosets
For $H\le G$ and $a,b\in G$: (1) $|aH|=|H|$; (2) $aH$ and $bH$ are either identical or disjoint; (3) the distinct left cosets of $H$ partition $G$.
:::

:::theorem Lagrange's Theorem
If $G$ is finite and $H\le G$, then $|H|$ divides $|G|$; specifically $|G|=[G:H]\cdot|H|$, where $[G:H]$ is the number of distinct left cosets of $H$.
:::

:::proof
By the coset properties, the distinct left cosets partition $G$ into $[G:H]$ blocks, each of size $|H|$ (property 1). Counting $|G|$ block by block: $|G| = [G:H]\cdot|H|$. Hence $|H|$ divides $|G|$. $\blacksquare$

**Why $|aH|=|H|$:** the map $h\mapsto a*h$ is a bijection from $H$ to $aH$ — it's injective because $a*h_1=a*h_2 \implies h_1=h_2$ (cancel $a$ using its inverse), and surjective onto $aH$ by definition.

**Why cosets are disjoint or equal:** if $x\in aH\cap bH$, then $x=a*h_1=b*h_2$ for some $h_1,h_2\in H$, so $a=b*h_2*h_1^{-1}\in bH$, giving $aH\subseteq bH$; the symmetric argument gives $bH\subseteq aH$, so $aH=bH$.
:::

## Worked examples

:::example Easy
$|S_3|=6$. By Lagrange's Theorem, any subgroup of $S_3$ has order in $\{1,2,3,6\}$ — orders $4$ and $5$ are impossible without checking a single subset.
:::

:::example Standard university level
Find $|H|$ dividing $12$ for subgroups of $\mathbb Z_{12}$: possible orders are $1,2,3,4,6,12$. Since $\mathbb Z_{12}$ is cyclic, it actually has exactly one subgroup of each divisor order (generated by $12/d$ for each divisor $d$) — Lagrange gives the necessary condition, and cyclic structure gives the full classification here.
:::

:::example Exam level
Prove: if $|G|=p$ (prime), then $G$ is cyclic. Take any $a\ne e$ in $G$; the subgroup $\langle a\rangle$ generated by $a$ has order dividing $p$ (Lagrange) and order $>1$ (since $a\ne e$), so $|\langle a\rangle|=p=|G|$, forcing $\langle a\rangle=G$. Hence $G$ is cyclic, generated by any non-identity element.
:::

:::example Difficult
Use Lagrange's Theorem to derive Fermat's Little Theorem: for prime $p$ and $a$ not divisible by $p$, $a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p$. The group $U(p)=\{1,2,\dots,p-1\}$ under multiplication mod $p$ has order $p-1$. For any $a\in U(p)$, the order of $a$ (as an element) divides $|U(p)|=p-1$ (a corollary of Lagrange applied to the cyclic subgroup $\langle a\rangle$), so $a^{p-1} = (a^{\text{ord}(a)})^{(p-1)/\text{ord}(a)} \equiv 1^{(p-1)/\text{ord}(a)} = 1 \pmod p$.
:::

:::counterexample
**Statement that looks true:** "If $d$ divides $|G|$, then $G$ has a subgroup of order $d$" (the converse of Lagrange's Theorem).

**Counterexample:** the alternating group $A_4$ has order $12$, and $6$ divides $12$, yet $A_4$ has **no** subgroup of order $6$.

**Why it fails:** Lagrange's Theorem is a one-directional necessary condition, not a characterization — divisibility of the order is necessary for a subgroup to exist, but never sufficient. (The full converse does hold for cyclic groups and, more generally, in restricted forms like the Sylow theorems, but not unconditionally.)
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake
Treating Lagrange's Theorem as if it guarantees a subgroup exists for every divisor of $|G|$ — as the $A_4$ counterexample shows, this converse is false in general. Lagrange only rules subgroup orders **out**, it never confirms one exists.
:::

## Connections to other topics

:::connection
This is the launching point for the isomorphism theorems and quotient groups (MTH304-CH04): the index $[G:H]$ from Lagrange's Theorem becomes the order of the quotient group $G/H$ once $H$ is normal.
:::

## Applications

:::application
**Cryptography:** Fermat's Little Theorem (derived above from Lagrange) is the mathematical foundation of the RSA public-key cryptosystem's correctness proof.
:::

## Exam-ready answer

:::examready
For finite $G$ and $H\le G$: $|G|=[G:H]\cdot|H|$, so $|H|$ divides $|G|$. Proof via cosets: they all have size $|H|$ (bijection $h\mapsto ah$) and partition $G$ (equal or disjoint). Immediate corollaries: every group of prime order is cyclic; the order of any element divides $|G|$; Fermat's Little Theorem. The converse (existence of a subgroup for every divisor) is **false** in general — cite $A_4$ (order 12, no subgroup of order 6).
:::

## Viva preparation

:::viva
**Q: Does Lagrange's Theorem tell you a subgroup of a given order exists?**
No — only that if a subgroup exists, its order divides $|G|$. The converse is false in general (counterexample: $A_4$).

**Q: Why does every group of prime order have to be cyclic?**
Because the order of any non-identity element's generated subgroup must divide the prime $p$, and can't be $1$, so it must equal $p$ — forcing that single element to generate the whole group.

**Follow-up:** What's the smallest counterexample to the converse of Lagrange's theorem?
$A_4$, order 12, with no subgroup of order 6 — the standard textbook example.
:::
