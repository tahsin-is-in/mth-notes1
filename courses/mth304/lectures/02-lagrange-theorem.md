## 2.1 Cosets

:::definition Coset
Let $H \leq G$ and $a \in G$. The **left coset** of $H$ determined by $a$ is
$$
aH = \{ a \ast h : h \in H \}.
$$
:::

:::theorem Properties of Cosets
For $H \leq G$ and $a, b \in G$:

1. $|aH| = |H|$ for every $a \in G$.
2. Two cosets $aH$ and $bH$ are either identical or disjoint.
3. The cosets of $H$ partition $G$.
:::

**Why this works.** Each coset $aH$ is in bijection with $H$ via $h \mapsto a \ast h$, which is injective by the cancellation law — this gives property 1. Property 2 follows because if $aH \cap bH \neq \emptyset$, some element lies in both, and a short computation shows $aH = bH$ exactly.

## 2.2 Lagrange's Theorem

:::theorem Lagrange's Theorem
If $G$ is a finite group and $H \leq G$, then $|H|$ divides $|G|$. In fact,
$$
|G| = [G : H] \cdot |H|,
$$
where $[G:H]$, the **index** of $H$ in $G$, is the number of distinct left cosets of $H$ in $G$.
:::

**Proof.** By the previous theorem, the distinct left cosets of $H$ partition $G$ into $[G:H]$ blocks, each of size $|H|$. Counting elements of $G$ block by block gives $|G| = [G:H]\cdot|H|$, so $|H|$ divides $|G|$. $\blacksquare$

:::example Applying Lagrange's Theorem
$S_3$ has order $6$. By Lagrange's Theorem, every subgroup of $S_3$ has order dividing $6$ — so only orders $1, 2, 3, 6$ are possible. Order $4$ or $5$ subgroups cannot exist, without checking a single element.
:::

:::important Exam tip
Lagrange's Theorem constrains what subgroup orders are *possible* — it does **not** guarantee a subgroup of every divisor order actually exists. Converse-type "does $G$ have a subgroup of order $k$" questions need a separate argument (or a counterexample), not just Lagrange's Theorem.
:::

## 2.3 An immediate corollary

If $|G| = p$ is prime, the only divisors of $p$ are $1$ and $p$, so $G$'s only subgroups are $\{e\}$ and $G$ itself — meaning **every group of prime order is cyclic**, generated by any non-identity element. This is a small but genuinely surprising consequence of a purely counting argument.
